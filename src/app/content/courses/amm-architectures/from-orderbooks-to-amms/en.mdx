# From Order Books to AMMs

Order books match buyers and sellers through lists of outstanding bids and asks. This model dominates traditional finance and centralized exchanges because it provides price discovery and tight spreads when liquidity is abundant.

On-chain order books face a problem: every order placement, cancellation, and modification requires a transaction. High-frequency market making becomes expensive or impossible. Automated Market Makers (AMMs) solve this by replacing the order book with a mathematical formula that determines prices based on pool reserves. This lesson covers both models and explains why AMMs became the dominant DEX architecture.

<ArticleSection name="The Order Book Model" id="order-book" level="h2" />

### How Order Books Work

An order book is a list of outstanding buy orders (bids) and sell orders (asks):

```
ASKS (Sell Orders)
$102.50  |  500 shares
$102.00  |  1,200 shares
$101.50  |  800 shares
--------------------------
$101.00  |  600 shares   ← Best bid
$100.50  |  2,000 shares
$100.00  |  1,500 shares
BIDS (Buy Orders)
```

The **spread** is the gap between best bid ($101.00) and best ask ($101.50). Market makers profit by buying at the bid and selling at the ask, providing liquidity and earning the spread.

### Why Order Books Dominate Traditional Finance

Order books are efficient:
- **Price discovery**: Prices emerge from actual supply and demand
- **Capital efficiency**: Makers only commit capital at specific prices
- **Precise execution**: Traders control exactly what price they get
- **Low slippage**: Deep books absorb large orders with minimal price impact

Every major exchange uses order books.

<ArticleSection name="The Order Book Problem" id="orderbook-problem" level="h2" />

### Order Books Need Market Makers

Order books don't fill themselves, someone has to post those bids and asks. In traditional finance, that's market makers: firms like Citadel or Jump Trading who quote prices, manage inventory, and profit from the spread.

Market making is a specialized, capital-intensive business. You need:
- Sophisticated pricing algorithms
- Fast infrastructure to update quotes
- Risk management systems
- Significant capital

For major assets (BTC, ETH, SOL), market makers show up so the spreads are tight, the books are deep.

But this isn't the case for the other 10,000 tokens and long-tail assets that no professional market maker wants to touch.

### The Ethereum Constraint

On Ethereum circa 2017-2020, order books had additional problems:
- **15-second blocks**: By the time your order confirms, price has moved
- **$1-50 gas fees**: Updating quotes costs real money
- **Public mempool**: Everyone sees your orders before execution

Early on-chain order books (EtherDelta, IDEX) were slow and illiquid.

### The Solana Reality

Solana changed this. With 400ms blocks and sub-cent fees, on-chain order books work.

But even here, AMMs dominate because the real innovation wasn't "order books for slow chains." 

It was the possibility of **democratizing market making** so anyone could deposit into a pool and earn fees turning anyone into an actual market maker.

<ArticleSection name="The AMM Breakthrough" id="amm-breakthrough" level="h2" />

### Bancor: The First On-Chain AMM (2017)

Bancor launched in June 2017 with the idea that they shouldn't match buyers and sellers, but hold reserves of tokens and used a formula to calculate prices.

Bancor introduced:
- **Bonding curves**: Mathematical functions that determine price based on supply
- **Continuous liquidity**: Always available, no need to wait for a match
- **Permissionless listing**: Any token could be traded without exchange approval

But Bancor had problems. The protocol was complex, required their native BNT token as an intermediary, and had centralized upgrade mechanisms.

### Uniswap V1: Simplicity Wins (November 2018)

Hayden Adams, a mechanical engineer who learned Solidity after being laid off, built Uniswap V1 in a few months. It launched with around 500 lines of code.

The insight was brutal simplicity: **x * y = k**

```
Token A reserve: x
Token B reserve: y
Constant: k = x * y

Price of A in terms of B = y / x
```

No complex bonding curves, no intermediary assets. Just two tokens and a constant product formula.

### Why Constant Product Works

The formula `x * y = k` creates elegant properties:

**1. Infinite liquidity** (theoretically): The curve never touches the axes. You can always trade, though prices become extreme.

**2. Self-adjusting prices**: As one token is bought, its price increases automatically.

**3. Arbitrage-driven accuracy**: If Uniswap price diverges from market price, arbitrageurs profit by correcting it so the pool stays in sync with external markets without requiring an oracle.

**4. Passive liquidity provision**: LPs deposit tokens and earn fees without active management.

<ArticleSection name="The DeFi Summer Explosion" id="defi-summer" level="h2" />

### Uniswap V2 (May 2020)

V2 added critical features:
- **ERC-20 pairs**: Trade any token against any token (not just ETH)
- **Price oracles**: Time-weighted average prices (TWAP) for other protocols
- **Flash swaps**: Borrow tokens, use them, repay in one transaction

### The Fork Wars

Uniswap's code was open source. Sushiswap forked it in August 2020, added a token, and tried to vampire attack Uniswap's liquidity.

This kicked off an era of rapid innovation. Every week brought new AMM designs, each trying to solve problems with the constant product formula:
- **Slippage**: Large trades move price significantly
- **Impermanent loss**: LPs lose value when prices diverge
- **Capital inefficiency**: Most liquidity sits unused at extreme prices

<ArticleSection name="The Limitations That Spawned Innovation" id="limitations" level="h2" />

### Slippage

In constant product AMMs, large trades move price significantly:

```
Pool: 100 ETH, 100,000 USDC (k = 10,000,000)
Price: 1 ETH = 1,000 USDC

Buy 10 ETH:
- New ETH reserve: 90
- New USDC reserve: 10,000,000 / 90 = 111,111
- Cost: 11,111 USDC for 10 ETH
- Effective price: 1,111 USDC/ETH (11% slippage!)
```

For stablecoins that should trade 1:1, this is absurd. Swapping USDC for USDT shouldn't have any slippage.

**Solution**: StableSwap (Curve): optimized for pegged assets.

### Capital Inefficiency

In `x * y = k`, liquidity is spread across all prices from 0 to infinity, but the reality is that most trading happens in a narrow range.

```
ETH/USDC pool with $10M liquidity
ETH price: $1,500

Liquidity actually useful for ±5% trades: ~$500K
Liquidity sitting unused: ~$9.5M
```

**Solution**: Concentrated Liquidity (Uniswap V3): LPs choose their price ranges.

### Impermanent Loss

When prices change, LPs end up with less value than if they'd just held:

```
Initial deposit: 1 ETH + 1,000 USDC = $2,000
ETH doubles to $2,000

If you held: 1 ETH ($2,000) + 1,000 USDC = $3,000
In the pool: 0.707 ETH ($1,414) + 1,414 USDC = $2,828

Impermanent loss: $172 (5.7%)
```

The more volatile the pair, the worse the loss.

**Solution**: Oracle-integrated AMMs (GMX model): use external prices to eliminate IL.

### Fixed Ratios

Constant product forces 50/50 value splits. But what if you want exposure to multiple assets in different ratios? An index fund of 60% BTC, 30% ETH, 10% SOL?

**Solution**: Weighted Pools (Balancer): custom ratios, multiple assets.

### The Pattern

Each AMM design optimizes for different tradeoffs:

| Design | Optimizes For | Sacrifices |
|--------|--------------|------------|
| Constant Product | Simplicity, any pair | Capital efficiency |
| StableSwap | Pegged assets | Volatile pair handling |
| Concentrated Liquidity | Capital efficiency | LP complexity, JIT MEV |
| Weighted Pools | Custom exposure | Concentrated liquidity |
| Oracle-Integrated | Zero IL | Oracle dependency |

There's no universal best. The right AMM depends on the assets, the users, and the use case.

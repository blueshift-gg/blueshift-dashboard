# Concentrated Liquidity

You deposit $10,000 into a SOL/USDC pool. SOL is at $100. How much of your capital is actually working for you?

In a constant product AMM: about $100. The rest is sitting at prices like $1 or $10,000, waiting for a scenario that will never happen.

Concentrated liquidity fixes this: instead of spreading your capital from zero to infinity, you pick a range: "I think SOL will trade between $80 and $120." Now your entire $10,000 works within that range earning the same fees but with 100x less capital required.

The tradeoff is that if price leaves your range, you will earn nothing. And if it leaves permanently, you've effectively "sold: at the worst time.

<ArticleSection name="The Capital Efficiency Problem" id="efficiency-problem" level="h2" />

### Where Does Your Liquidity Actually Go?

In constant product AMMs, the `x * y = k` formula spreads liquidity across all possible prices:

```
SOL/USDC pool: $10M liquidity
Current price: $100/SOL

Liquidity allocated at:
- $1 - $10: ~$50K (will SOL ever be $5? Probably not)
- $10 - $50: ~$200K (unlikely)
- $50 - $80: ~$500K (possible but rare)
- $80 - $120: ~$2M ← Where trading actually happens
- $120 - $150: ~$500K
- $150 - $500: ~$300K
- $500+: ~$200K (SOL at $1000? Maybe someday)
```

Only ~20% of the liquidity is near the current price. The rest is waiting for extreme scenarios.

### The Utilization Math

```
$10M total liquidity
±5% of current price: ~$400K active liquidity
Utilization: 4%

96% of LP capital sits idle, earning zero fees.
```

This is why constant product LPs earn low APYs. Their capital isn't being used.

Concentrated liquidity lets you say: "I'll provide liquidity only between $80 and $120." If price stays there, you earn fees on 100% of your deposit.

<ArticleSection name="The V3 Model" id="v3-model" level="h2" />

### Positions Instead of Pool Shares

In constant product, all LPs share one pool and get fungible LP tokens. In concentrated liquidity, each LP has a distinct position with its own price range:

```
Position 1: $50K liquidity from $90 - $110
Position 2: $20K liquidity from $75 - $125
Position 3: $100K liquidity from $95 - $105 (very tight)
```

Positions overlap. At $100, all three are active and share fees proportionally. This creates deeper liquidity at popular price points.

### Active vs Inactive

A position only earns fees when price is within its range:

```
Current SOL price: $100

Position A ($90 - $110): ACTIVE - earning fees
Position B ($115 - $140): INACTIVE - waiting
Position C ($60 - $85): INACTIVE - waiting
```

If SOL pumps to $120, Position A stops earning and Position B starts. This is the fundamental tradeoff: concentration vs coverage.

### The Composition Shift

As price moves within your range, your position's composition changes:

```
Position: $90 - $110, $10K initial value

At $90 (lower bound): 100% SOL, 0% USDC
At $100 (middle): ~50% SOL, ~50% USDC  
At $110 (upper bound): 0% SOL, 100% USDC
```

Think about what this means. As SOL rises toward $110, you're gradually selling SOL for USDC. At $110, you hold only USDC. If SOL keeps rising to $150, you've sold all your SOL at an average price of ~$100 and are earning zero fees.

This is impermanent loss amplified. You sold the thing that went up.

<ArticleSection name="Ticks and Tick Spacing" id="ticks" level="h2" />

Since the new formula doesn't allow for arbitrary price ranges, prices are divided into discrete "ticks":

```
Each tick represents a 0.01% (1 basis point) price change.

Tick 0 = price 1.0000
Tick 1 = price 1.0001
Tick 100 = price 1.0100
Tick -100 = price 0.9901
```

The formula:

```
price(tick) = 1.0001^tick
tick(price) = log(price) / log(1.0001)
```

### Why Ticks?

Think about what "any price" means. Between $1,000 and $1,001, there are infinite possible prices: $1,000.001, $1,000.0001, $1,000.00001... You can't store liquidity at infinite price points.

Ticks solve this by discretizing price space:
- **Storage efficiency**: Finite set of price points that can be tracked on-chain.
- **Aggregation**: Multiple positions ending at the same tick share storage, reducing account sizes.
- **Precision**: 1 basis point (0.01%) spacing is fine for most use cases. 

### Tick Spacing

Not every tick is usable. "Tick spacing" determines which ticks are valid:

| Fee Tier | Tick Spacing | Price Increment |
|----------|--------------|-----------------|
| 0.01% (stables) | 1 | 0.01% |
| 0.05% | 10 | 0.10% |
| 0.30% | 60 | 0.60% |
| 1.00% | 200 | 2.00% |

Volatile pairs don't need 0.01% precision. Using every 200th tick instead of every tick means fewer accounts to traverse during swaps.

### The Tick Bitmap

To use tick efficiently, we use a bitmap to track which ticks have liquidity:

```rust
// Bitmap: each bit represents whether a tick is initialized
// Position in bitmap = tick_index / 256
// Bit position = tick_index % 256

fn is_tick_initialized(tick_bitmap: &[u256], tick: i32) -> bool {
    let word_pos = tick >> 8; // tick / 256
    let bit_pos = tick % 256;
    let word = tick_bitmap[word_pos as usize];
    (word >> bit_pos) & 1 == 1
}
```

This way, when swapping, the contract only needs to check initialized ticks, not every possible price.

<ArticleSection name="The Math" id="math" level="h2" />

### Virtual Reserves

Within a tick range, V3 still uses `x * y = k`, but with "virtual" reserves:

```
Real reserves: what the position actually holds
Virtual reserves: what reserves would be if liquidity extended to 0 and ∞
```

> Concentrated liquidity behaves like a larger `x * y = k` pool within its range.

### Liquidity (L)

Liquidity is defined as:

```
L = sqrt(x * y)

Where:
- L = liquidity
- x = virtual reserve of token X
- y = virtual reserve of token Y
```

L remains constant as swaps occur (within a tick range), only the ratio of x to y changes.

### Price

The new formula uses `sqrt(price)` internally:

```
√P = sqrt(price of token1 in terms of token0)

Price = (√P)²
```

This simplifies the math for liquidity calculations:

```
x = L / √P  (amount of token0)
y = L * √P  (amount of token1)
```

### The Swap Formula

For a swap within a single tick range:

```
Δ√P = Δy / L    (when swapping token0 for token1)
Δ√P = Δx * L    (when swapping token1 for token0)

Where:
- Δ√P = change in sqrt price
- Δy = amount of token1 added
- Δx = amount of token0 added
- L = liquidity
```

But this is not always the case, it might happen that we need to cross ticks, or traversing a position, during a swap.

A position between price_lower and price_upper contributes:

```
token0_amount = L * (1/√P_current - 1/√P_upper)
token1_amount = L * (√P_current - √P_lower)

Only when: P_lower ≤ P_current ≤ P_upper
```

So when a swap pushes price past an initialized tick, liquidity changes:

```
Price moving from $2,000 to $2,100

At tick for $2,050:
- Position A ($1,900-$2,050) ends: remove its liquidity
- Position B ($2,050-$2,200) begins: add its liquidity

Total liquidity = Σ(all active positions)
```

This means that we have to account for all ticks after, or before the current one during swaps

```rust
fn swap(amount_in: u128, zero_for_one: bool) -> u128 {
    let mut amount_remaining = amount_in;
    let mut amount_out = 0u128;
    let mut current_tick = self.current_tick;
    let mut current_sqrt_price = self.sqrt_price;
    let mut current_liquidity = self.liquidity;
    
    while amount_remaining > 0 {
        // Find next initialized tick
        let next_tick = self.next_initialized_tick(current_tick, zero_for_one);
        let next_sqrt_price = tick_to_sqrt_price(next_tick);
        
        // Compute swap within this tick range
        let (amount_in_step, amount_out_step, sqrt_price_next) = 
            compute_swap_step(
                current_sqrt_price,
                next_sqrt_price,
                current_liquidity,
                amount_remaining,
            );
        
        amount_remaining -= amount_in_step;
        amount_out += amount_out_step;
        current_sqrt_price = sqrt_price_next;
        
        // If we reached the next tick, cross it
        if sqrt_price_next == next_sqrt_price {
            current_liquidity = self.cross_tick(next_tick);
            current_tick = if zero_for_one { next_tick - 1 } else { next_tick };
        }
    }
    
    // Update state
    self.sqrt_price = current_sqrt_price;
    self.current_tick = current_tick;
    self.liquidity = current_liquidity;
    
    amount_out
}
```

### Compute and Account Implications

Each tick crossing requires loading and updating tick state. On Solana, this means:

```
Small swap (within one tick range): 1 tick array account
Large swap (crossing tick array boundaries): 2-3 tick array accounts
Very large swap (crossing many boundaries): 4+ accounts

More accounts = more compute units = potential transaction failure
```

This is why Solana implementations like Orca Whirlpools pre-calculate the accounts needed and why Jupiter routes through multiple hops if a single swap would cross too many ticks.

<ArticleSection name="Impermanent Loss Amplified" id="impermanent-loss" level="h2" />

### Concentrated IL

Concentrated liquidity amplifies impermanent loss within the range:

```
Full range position (like V2):
- 2x price change → 5.7% IL

Position from P to 2P (concentrated):
- 2x price change → position entirely in one token
- If price returns: no loss
- If price doesn't return: effectively sold at average of range
```

### The Math

For a position between prices P_a and P_b:

```
IL = 2 * sqrt(P_current/P_initial) / (1 + P_current/P_initial) - 1

Concentrated IL multiplier ≈ sqrt(P_b/P_a)
```

Tighter range = higher multiplier = more IL if price exits range.

### Out-of-Range Positions

When price exits your range:

```
Position: $90 - $110
SOL drops to $70

Result:
- Position holds 100% SOL (bought all the way down)
- Earning zero fees (out of range)
- Waiting for price to return
```

You've effectively placed a limit order. Price returning means you profit but if price stay low means you're holding a depreciating asset.

<ArticleSection name="JIT Liquidity and MEV" id="jit-mev" level="h2" />

### Just-In-Time Liquidity

Concentrated liquidity created a new MEV opportunity. Here's how it works:

```
1. Searcher sees large swap pending (via RPC or Jito bundles)
2. Adds concentrated liquidity right at current price
3. Swap executes against their liquidity (they earn the fees)
4. Removes liquidity in same transaction bundle
```

The JIT provider captures fees that would have gone to long-term LPs. Since they're in and out instantly, they take zero impermanent loss.

### A Concrete Example

```
SOL/USDC pool at $100
Long-term LP: $100K liquidity from $90 - $110
JIT LP: $100K liquidity from $99.95 - $100.05

Large swap comes in at $100:
- JIT position is 200x more concentrated
- Captures ~99% of the fees from that swap
- Long-term LP gets scraps
```

The long-term LP provided capital for weeks but the JIT LP showed up for one transaction and took most of the reward.

<ArticleSection name="Active Liquidity Management" id="management" level="h2" />

### The LP's Dilemma

Concentrated liquidity requires active management:

```
Set and forget (wide range):
- Lower capital efficiency
- Lower fees per dollar
- Less IL risk
- Simpler

Active management (tight range):
- Higher capital efficiency
- Higher fees per dollar
- Higher IL risk if price moves
- Requires constant attention
```

### Rebalancing Strategies

**Range orders**: Set asymmetric ranges expecting directional moves.

**Mean reversion**: Keep range centered on TWAP, rebalance when price deviates.

**Volatility-based**: Widen ranges during high volatility, tighten during calm.

### Automated Vaults

There are certain protocols that automate concentrated liquidity management:
- Algorithms rebalance positions
- Single deposit, automatic range management
- Trade-off: vault fees + smart contract risk

<ArticleSection name="Key Takeaways" id="takeaways" level="h2" />

1. **Concentrated liquidity** allows LPs to focus capital in specific price ranges
2. **Up to 4000x capital efficiency** for very tight ranges
3. **Ticks** discretize price space; **tick spacing** varies by fee tier
4. **Virtual reserves** make the math work within ranges
5. **IL is amplified** when price exits your range
6. **JIT liquidity** is a new MEV vector unique to concentrated liquidity
7. **Active management** is required to maximize returns

Concentrated liquidity revolutionized capital efficiency but shifted complexity to LPs. It's the dominant design for high-volume pairs, but not universally superior.

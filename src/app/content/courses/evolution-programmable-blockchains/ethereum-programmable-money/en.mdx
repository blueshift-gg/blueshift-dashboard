# What is Ethereum? Smart Contracts and Programmable Money Explained

[Bitcoin](/courses/blockchain-fundamentals/bitcoin-first-blockchain) proved blockchain works for money. But what if you wanted blockchain to do more—loans, trades, organizations, games? [Bitcoin's limited scripting language](/courses/blockchain-fundamentals/bitcoin-first-blockchain#bitcoin-design-philosophy) makes this nearly impossible.

Ethereum is a blockchain that runs smart contracts—programs that execute on every node and maintain state without central authority. This transformed blockchain from a payment network into a general-purpose computer for decentralized applications.

<ArticleSection name="The Vision: A World Computer" id="vision-world-computer" level="h2" />

Bitcoin only transfers value. The protocol supports basic conditions like multi-signature wallets and time locks, but cannot implement complex logic.

In 2013, Vitalik Buterin proposed extending blockchain beyond payments. If blockchain can maintain state without central authority, it could run any computation without central authority. Not just money transfers—any program that needs verifiable, unstoppable execution.

Ethereum launched in 2015 as a blockchain that executes arbitrary code. Developers write programs called smart contracts that live on the blockchain. Anyone can interact with these programs by sending transactions. The entire network executes the code and agrees on the result.

<ArticleSection name="Smart Contracts" id="smart-contracts" level="h2" />

Smart contracts are programs that live on the blockchain and maintain state between executions.

Traditional contracts require lawyers and courts to enforce terms. Smart contracts enforce themselves through code. The agreement is executable logic. The blockchain guarantees execution without trusted third parties.

Consider a simple example: an escrow service. Alice wants to buy Bob's laptop for 1 ETH, but neither trusts the other. In the traditional system, they use an escrow service that holds Alice's money until Bob delivers the laptop.

With a smart contract:
1. Alice sends 1 ETH to the escrow contract
2. The contract holds the funds
3. When Alice confirms she received the laptop, the contract sends 1 ETH to Bob
4. If Alice does not confirm within 30 days, the contract refunds Alice

The contract executes exactly as written. No company can freeze funds, change terms, or disappear with the money.

This capability enabled applications Bitcoin cannot support. Decentralized exchanges trade tokens without intermediaries. Lending protocols use algorithmic interest rates. Stablecoins maintain pegged values through on-chain collateral. NFT marketplaces handle unique digital assets. DAOs coordinate treasury spending through token-weighted voting.

<ArticleSection name="The Account Model" id="account-model" level="h2" />

Ethereum replaced Bitcoin's UTXO system with an account-based model. Instead of tracking individual coins, Ethereum tracks account balances like a traditional bank.

In Ethereum, there are two types of accounts:

**Externally Owned Accounts (EOAs):** Controlled by users with private keys, similar to Bitcoin addresses. They have a balance and can send transactions. When you use MetaMask or another Ethereum wallet, you control an EOA.

**Contract Accounts:** Controlled by code, not private keys. They have a balance AND store executable code plus persistent data. Smart contracts live in contract accounts.

The account model enables persistent state—data that survives across transactions. A smart contract remembers information from previous interactions, maintains complex data structures, and evolves over time.

A lending protocol stores all outstanding loans in its contract account. It tracks interest rates, collateral amounts, and liquidation thresholds. When you repay a loan, the contract updates its state to reflect the repayment. This state persists indefinitely on the blockchain.

Bitcoin's UTXO model would require complex workarounds to achieve similar functionality.

<ArticleSection name="The Ethereum Virtual Machine" id="ethereum-virtual-machine" level="h2" />

The Ethereum Virtual Machine (EVM) runs on every node and executes smart contracts. It defines what programs can run, how they execute, and what resources they consume.

The EVM is a stack-based virtual machine that executes bytecode. Developers write smart contracts in high-level languages like Solidity, which compile to EVM bytecode. Every Ethereum node runs the same virtual machine, ensuring every node gets the same result from the same code.

The EVM is completely deterministic. Given the same input and state, it always produces the same output. If different nodes could get different results from the same code, the network could not reach consensus.

Smart contracts run in a sandboxed environment. A contract cannot access your computer's files, make network requests to external servers, or affect anything outside the blockchain. This prevents contracts from attacking nodes or users.

Every operation costs gas—a measure of computational resources. Simple operations like addition cost 3 gas. Reading from storage costs 200 gas. Complex operations cost more. This prevents infinite loops and ensures miners are compensated for computation.

<ArticleSection name="Gas: Paying for Computation" id="gas" level="h2" />

Gas is Ethereum's mechanism for measuring and paying for computational work.

When you send an Ethereum transaction, you specify:
- **Gas limit:** Maximum gas you are willing to spend
- **Gas price:** How much you will pay per unit of gas (in gwei, where 1 gwei = 0.000000001 ETH)

Your maximum transaction cost is `gas limit × gas price`. If the transaction uses less gas than the limit, you get refunded the difference. If it needs more gas than the limit, the transaction reverts and you pay for the gas used up to the limit.

Gas prevents infinite loops. Without it, someone could deploy a contract with an infinite loop that never terminates. Miners would be stuck processing it forever. Gas ensures every computation has a maximum cost. When gas runs out, execution stops.

Gas compensates miners. Miners use computational resources to execute contracts. Gas fees pay them for this work. More complex contracts cost more to run.

Gas creates market-based pricing. When many people want to transact simultaneously, gas prices increase. Users who pay higher gas prices get their transactions processed first. Users bid for block space through gas prices.

A simple ETH transfer costs 21,000 gas. If gas price is 50 gwei, the transaction costs 0.00105 ETH (about $2 when ETH is $2,000). A complex smart contract interaction might cost 200,000 gas, costing 0.01 ETH (about $20).

The EVM processes transactions sequentially. When network demand exceeds capacity, gas prices spike dramatically.

<ArticleSection name="Composability: Money Legos" id="composability" level="h2" />

Smart contracts can call other smart contracts within the same transaction. This composability creates "money legos"—building complex financial applications by combining simple components.

Consider this transaction flow:
1. Borrow USDC from lending protocol A
2. Trade USDC for ETH on decentralized exchange B
3. Provide ETH as liquidity to lending protocol C
4. Use the receipt token as collateral on protocol D
5. Borrow more assets to repeat the process

All five steps execute in a single atomic transaction. Either everything succeeds or everything reverts—no partial states. If step 4 fails, the entire transaction undoes, and you are back where you started.

This enables arbitrage, flash loans, and complex trading strategies impossible in traditional finance. Developers build on existing protocols rather than rebuilding functionality from scratch.

<ArticleSection name="What Ethereum Unlocked" id="what-ethereum-unlocked" level="h2" />

Smart contracts enabled applications Bitcoin could not support.

Decentralized Finance (DeFi) protocols handle lending, borrowing, and trading without banks. Uniswap, Aave, and MakerDAO manage billions of dollars in value entirely through smart contracts.

Non-Fungible Tokens (NFTs) are unique digital assets with provable ownership. They represent art, collectibles, event tickets, in-game items, and digital identity.

Decentralized Autonomous Organizations (DAOs) coordinate through token holder voting. They have no CEO, no board, and no traditional corporate structure. Treasury and operations are governed through code.

Stablecoins maintain stable value against fiat currencies. USDC and DAI let crypto users avoid volatility without exiting to traditional banks.

Prediction markets let users bet on future events with transparent, permissionless market making.

Ethereum proved blockchains can support general-purpose computation. But success revealed a problem: as usage grew, transactions became slower and more expensive. High gas prices during peak usage reflect market pricing of scarce block space, which raises [the question of why block space remains scarce](/courses/evolution-programmable-blockchains/scalability-challenge).

# Ethereum: Programmable Money

Bitcoin proved blockchain works for money. But what if you wanted blockchain to do more—loans, trades, organizations, games? Bitcoin's design makes this nearly impossible. Ethereum asked: what if the blockchain could run any program?

"Blockchain running programs" means smart contracts. Ethereum transformed blockchain from a payment network into a general-purpose computer.

<ArticleSection name="The Vision: A World Computer" id="vision-world-computer" level="h2" />

Bitcoin's limitation: it only transfers value. The protocol supports basic conditions like multi-signature wallets and time locks, but cannot implement complex logic.

Vitalik Buterin recognized a bigger opportunity. If blockchain can maintain state without central authority, it could run any computation without central authority. Not just money transfers—any program that needs to be verifiable, unstoppable, and censorship-resistant.

Ethereum launched in 2015 with this vision: a blockchain that executes arbitrary code. Developers write programs called smart contracts that live on the blockchain. Anyone can interact with these programs by sending transactions. The entire network executes the code and agrees on the result.

<ArticleSection name="Smart Contracts" id="smart-contracts" level="h2" />

Smart contracts are programs that live on the blockchain and maintain state between executions.

Unlike traditional contracts requiring lawyers and courts to enforce terms, smart contracts enforce themselves through code. The agreement exists as executable logic. The blockchain ensures execution without requiring anyone to trust a third party.

Consider a simple example: an escrow service. Alice wants to buy Bob's laptop for 1 ETH, but neither trusts the other. In the traditional system, they use an escrow service that holds Alice's money until Bob delivers the laptop.

With a smart contract:
1. Alice sends 1 ETH to the escrow contract
2. The contract holds the funds
3. When Alice confirms she received the laptop, the contract sends 1 ETH to Bob
4. If Alice does not confirm within 30 days, the contract refunds Alice

The contract executes exactly as written. No company can freeze funds, change terms, or disappear with the money. The code is the authority.

Smart contracts unlock applications impossible on Bitcoin:
- **Decentralized exchanges:** Trade tokens without intermediaries
- **Lending protocols:** Borrow and lend assets with algorithmic interest rates
- **Stablecoins:** Cryptocurrencies designed to maintain stable value
- **NFT marketplaces:** Trade unique digital assets
- **Decentralized autonomous organizations (DAOs):** Organizations governed by token holders without traditional corporate structure

<ArticleSection name="The Account Model" id="account-model" level="h2" />

Ethereum replaced Bitcoin's UTXO system with an account-based model. Instead of tracking individual coins, Ethereum tracks account balances like a traditional bank.

In Ethereum, there are two types of accounts:

**Externally Owned Accounts (EOAs):** Controlled by users with private keys, similar to Bitcoin addresses. They have a balance and can send transactions. When you use MetaMask or another Ethereum wallet, you control an EOA.

**Contract Accounts:** Controlled by code, not private keys. They have a balance AND store executable code plus persistent data. Smart contracts live in contract accounts.

This account model enables persistent state—data that survives across transactions. A smart contract can remember information from previous interactions, maintain complex data structures, and evolve over time.

Consider a lending protocol:
- The contract account stores all outstanding loans
- It remembers interest rates, collateral amounts, and liquidation thresholds
- When you repay a loan, the contract updates its state to reflect the repayment
- This state persists indefinitely on the blockchain

The account model makes this natural. Bitcoin's UTXO model would require complex workarounds to achieve similar functionality.

<ArticleSection name="The Ethereum Virtual Machine" id="ethereum-virtual-machine" level="h2" />

All of this is possible thanks to the Ethereum Virtual Machine (EVM), which runs on every node and makes the blockchain programmable. It defines what programs can run, how they execute, and what resources they consume.

The EVM is a stack-based virtual machine that executes bytecode. Developers write smart contracts in high-level languages like Solidity, which compile to EVM bytecode. Every Ethereum node runs the same virtual machine, ensuring every node gets the same result from the same code.

**Deterministic execution:** The EVM is completely deterministic. Given the same input and state, it always produces the same output. This property is critical—if different nodes could get different results from the same code, the network could not reach consensus.

**Isolation:** Smart contracts run in a sandboxed environment. A contract cannot access your computer's files, make network requests to external servers, or affect anything outside the blockchain. This isolation prevents contracts from attacking nodes or users.

**Gas metering:** Every operation costs gas—a measure of computational resources. Simple operations like addition cost 3 gas. Reading from storage costs 200 gas. Complex operations cost more. This prevents infinite loops and ensures miners are compensated for computation.

<ArticleSection name="Gas: Paying for Computation" id="gas" level="h2" />

Gas is Ethereum's mechanism for measuring and paying for computational work.

When you send an Ethereum transaction, you specify:
- **Gas limit:** Maximum gas you are willing to spend
- **Gas price:** How much you will pay per unit of gas (in gwei, where 1 gwei = 0.000000001 ETH)

Your maximum transaction cost is `gas limit × gas price`. If the transaction uses less gas than the limit, you get refunded the difference. If it needs more gas than the limit, the transaction reverts and you pay for the gas used up to the limit.

Gas serves multiple purposes:

**Prevents infinite loops:** Without gas, someone could deploy a contract with an infinite loop that never terminates. Miners would be stuck processing it forever. Gas ensures every computation has a maximum cost. When gas runs out, execution stops.

**Compensates miners:** Miners use computational resources to execute contracts. Gas fees compensate them for this work. More complex contracts cost more to run, so they pay more.

**Market-based pricing:** When many people want to transact simultaneously, gas prices increase. Users who pay higher gas prices get their transactions processed first. This creates a fee market where users bid for block space.

**Example:** A simple ETH transfer costs 21,000 gas. If gas price is 50 gwei, the transaction costs 0.00105 ETH (about $2 when ETH is $2,000). A complex smart contract interaction might cost 200,000 gas, costing 0.01 ETH (about $20).

The EVM processes transactions one at a time, creating a bottleneck. When network demand exceeds capacity, gas prices spike dramatically.

<ArticleSection name="Composability: Money Legos" id="composability" level="h2" />

Smart contracts can call other smart contracts within the same transaction. This composability creates "money legos"—building complex financial applications by combining simple components.

Consider this transaction flow:
1. Borrow USDC from lending protocol A
2. Trade USDC for ETH on decentralized exchange B
3. Provide ETH as liquidity to lending protocol C
4. Use the receipt token as collateral on protocol D
5. Borrow more assets to repeat the process

All five steps execute in a single atomic transaction. Either everything succeeds or everything reverts—no partial states. If step 4 fails, the entire transaction undoes, and you are back where you started.

Smart contract composability enables arbitrage, flash loans, and complex trading strategies impossible in traditional finance. Developers build on existing protocols rather than rebuilding functionality from scratch. The entire ecosystem becomes greater than the sum of its parts.

<ArticleSection name="What Ethereum Unlocked" id="what-ethereum-unlocked" level="h2" />

Smart contracts made blockchain useful for thousands of applications.

**Decentralized Finance (DeFi):** Lending, borrowing, trading, and earning interest without banks. Protocols like Uniswap, Aave, and MakerDAO handle billions of dollars in value entirely through smart contracts.

**Non-Fungible Tokens (NFTs):** Unique digital assets with provable ownership. Art, collectibles, event tickets, in-game items, and digital identity.

**Decentralized Autonomous Organizations (DAOs):** Organizations where token holders vote on decisions. No traditional corporate structure. No CEO or board. Governance through code.

**Stablecoins:** Cryptocurrencies designed to maintain stable value against fiat currencies. USDC and DAI enable crypto users to avoid volatility without exiting to traditional banks.

**Prediction markets:** Bet on future events with transparent, permissionless market making.

Ethereum demonstrated that blockchains could support general-purpose computation. But success revealed a fundamental problem: as more people used Ethereum, transactions became slower and more expensive. High gas prices during peak usage reflect market pricing of scarce block space, which raises the question of why block space remains scarce.

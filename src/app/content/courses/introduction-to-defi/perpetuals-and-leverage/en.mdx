# Perpetuals & Leverage

March 2020. Bitcoin drops 50% in 24 hours. Traders with 100x leverage on BitMEX watch $1 billion in positions get liquidated. The liquidations create more selling, which triggers more liquidations. The exchange goes offline for "maintenance" as the cascade spirals.

This is the world of perpetual futures: instruments so powerful they can multiply your returns 100x, or vaporize your capital in minutes.

Perpetuals are now the dominant way crypto is traded; on most days, perp volume exceeds spot volume by 3-5x. This is why understanding how they work isn't optional if you're building DeFi.

### What Makes Perpetuals Different

Traditional futures expire: buy a December SOL future, and in December you settle. 

The expiry forces futures price to converge with spot; but since perpetuals never expire, what keeps the perp price aligned with spot?

**Funding rates**: periodic payments between longs and shorts that create economic pressure toward the real price.

<ArticleSection name="Leverage" id="leverage" level="h2" />

If you think SOL will hit $200 but you have $1,000, buying spot at $100 will get you 10 SOL. If SOL doubles, you make $1,000; A 100% return.

With leverage, for example 10x, with $1,000 you can actually control $10,000 worth of SOL. In that occasion, if SOL doubles, you make $10,000; A 1000% return.

The catch is that if SOL drops only 10%, your entire $1,000 is now worth zero.

```
$1,000 margin × 10x leverage = $10,000 exposure

Price +10%: $10,000 × 10% = +$1,000 (100% gain)
Price -10%: $10,000 × -10% = -$1,000 (100% loss = liquidated)
```

> Leverage amplifies everything. This is why perp protocols have the most complex risk management in DeFi.

<ArticleSection name="Position Mechanics" id="position-mechanics" level="h2" />

### Where Does the Perp Price Come From?

**Order Book** (dYdX, Hyperliquid)

Traders post limit orders so if you want to long you can buy from whoever's selling.

```
Order book for SOL-PERP:
  Asks: $100.50 (10 SOL), $100.60 (25 SOL), $100.75 (50 SOL)
  Bids: $100.40 (15 SOL), $100.30 (30 SOL), $100.20 (40 SOL)

You market buy 20 SOL:
  10 @ $100.50 + 10 @ $100.60 = avg price $100.55
```

Price = wherever supply meets demand. Needs market makers to keep the book filled.

**Virtual AMM** (Drift)

To price it there is no real order book but the protocol simulates an AMM curve:

```
Virtual reserves: 1,000,000 SOL × 100,000,000 USDC
k = 100,000,000,000,000

You long 100 SOL:
  New reserves: 999,900 SOL × 100,010,001 USDC
  Your entry price: $100.10
```

The curve moves based on net position flow. This means that if there are heavy long bias, the price can diverge from spot. The upside is that there are no LPs needed.

**Oracle-Based** (GMX, Jupiter Perps)

Price comes straight from Pyth/Switchboard. You get the oracle price, period.

```
Pyth says SOL = $100.00
You long 100 SOL at exactly $100.00
No slippage, no spread
```

The main problem is that there must be someone that take the other side. 

This is usually solved by implementing protocol liquidity pools like (GLP, JLP) that act as the counterparty to every trade and profits from traders going bust.

### The Position

A position tracks your bet:

```rust
#[account]
pub struct Position {
    pub owner: Pubkey,
    pub size: i64,           // Positive = long, negative = short
    pub entry_price: u64,
    pub margin: u64,         // Your collateral
    pub last_funding_index: i128,
}
```

### PnL

How much did price move since you entered?

```rust
pub fn calculate_pnl(position: &Position, current_price: u64) -> i64 {
    let price_diff = current_price as i64 - position.entry_price as i64;
    position.size * price_diff / PRICE_PRECISION as i64
}
```

The sign of `size` handles direction automatically:

```
Long 10 SOL at $100, price → $110:  10 × (110-100) = +$100
Short 10 SOL at $100, price → $90: -10 × (90-100) = +$100
Long 10 SOL at $100, price → $90:  10 × (90-100)  = -$100
```

But PnL isn't the whole story. Funding payments accumulate over time so a position can be profitable on price but actually underwater after funding.

<ArticleSection name="Margin" id="margin" level="h2" />

### Initial vs Maintenance

There are 2 types of fees when opening a position on perps:
- **Initial margin**: What you need to open. 10x max leverage = 10% of notional.
- **Maintenance margin**: Minimum to stay open. Usually lower (5%) to give breathing room.

```rust
// $10,000 position
// Initial margin (10%): $1,000 to open
// Maintenance margin (5%): $500 minimum to avoid liquidation
```

The gap between 10% and 5% means you can open a position and survive small price moves without immediate liquidation.

### Margin Ratio

Your health check: `equity / notional`

```rust
pub fn margin_ratio(position: &Position, price: u64) -> u64 {
    let pnl = calculate_pnl(position, price);
    let equity = position.margin as i64 + pnl;
    
    if equity <= 0 { return 0; }
    
    let notional = position.size.abs() as u64 * price / PRICE_PRECISION;
    equity as u64 * 10_000 / notional  // Returns BPS
}
```

When margin ratio drops below maintenance (500 bps), you're liquidatable.

### Cross vs Isolated

There are 2 types of assets that can be used as collateral in perps:
- **Isolated**: Each position has its own collateral. One liquidation doesn't affect others.
- **Cross**: All positions share your account balance. More capital efficient, but one bad trade can wipe everything.

Most perp DEXs support both. Cross is more complex but traders prefer it.

<ArticleSection name="Funding Rates" id="funding" level="h2" />

As we said in the introduction, traditional futures have a built-in price anchor: expiry. 

As expiry approaches, the gap between the payout for opening a short and the spot price closes since it becomes less risky. The future *must* converge to spot.

Since Perpetuals never expire, there's no delivery date forcing convergence. For this reason, without some mechanism, perp price could drift arbitrarily far from spot. 

For example a SOL perp could trade at $150 while spot is $100, and there's no natural force pulling them together.

### The Solution: Make Divergence Expensive

Funding rates create artificial convergence pressure by making it costly to hold positions that push price away from spot.

The rule is simple:
- **Perp > Spot**: Longs pay shorts
- **Perp < Spot**: Shorts pay longs

The further the divergence, the higher the payment.

### Why This Creates Convergence

Say SOL perp is at $105 while spot is $100. Longs must pay shorts.

**For existing longs**: Holding becomes expensive. Some close their positions (selling pressure → price drops).

**For potential shorts**: Free money! Short the perp, collect funding from longs. New shorts enter (selling pressure → price drops).

**For arbitrageurs**: Short perp at $105, buy spot at $100. Collect funding while you wait. When prices converge, close both positions. Profit = funding collected + any convergence gains.

All three forces push the perp price down toward spot.

The reverse happens when perp < spot. Shorts pay longs, so shorts close and new longs enter. Price gets pushed up.

### The Math

```rust
pub fn funding_rate(mark_price: u64, index_price: u64) -> i64 {
    // Funding proportional to the price gap
    let diff = mark_price as i64 - index_price as i64;
    diff * FUNDING_FACTOR / index_price as i64
    // Positive = longs pay shorts
    // Negative = shorts pay longs
}

pub fn funding_payment(position_size: i64, rate: i64) -> i64 {
    // Longs (positive size) pay when rate is positive
    // Shorts (negative size) pay when rate is negative
    position_size * rate / PRECISION as i64
}
```

### A Concrete Example

```
SOL perp: $105, spot: $100 (5% premium)
You're long 100 SOL = $10,500 notional

Funding rate: (105 - 100) / 100 = 5% per period
Funding payment: $10,500 × 5% = $525 every 8 hours

That's $1,575/day just to hold the position.
```

At these rates, either you close (don't want to pay), or someone shorts against you (wants to collect). Both actions push the perp price back toward spot.

<ArticleSection name="Mark vs Index Price" id="mark-index" level="h2" />

Two prices matter:

**Index price**: The "true" spot price from oracles. What SOL actually trades for.

**Mark price**: Used for PnL and liquidations. A blend of index and perp price, smoothed to resist manipulation.

Why separate? Imagine a flash crash: SOL drops from $100 to $50 for 30 seconds (fat finger, thin liquidity). If liquidations used raw spot price, thousands of positions would be wiped instantly. Price recovers to $98, but those traders are already liquidated.

```rust
// Mark price: blend index with perp price
pub fn mark_price(index: u64, perp: u64) -> u64 {
    (index * 7 + perp * 3) / 10
}
```

Mark price protects against flash crashes, oracle manipulation, and thin order books. But if it diverges too far from reality, traders can show profit on screen while actually being underwater.

<ArticleSection name="Liquidations" id="liquidations" level="h2" />

When margin ratio falls below maintenance, anyone can liquidate you. They close your position, take a bonus, and your remaining margin (if any) covers the loss.

### The Cascade

This is what March 2020 looked like:

```
Market drops 5%
→ 20x longs hit liquidation
→ Liquidations = market sells
→ Price drops another 3%
→ 15x longs now liquidatable
→ More liquidations, more selling
→ 10x longs hit
→ Cascade until buying absorbs the pressure
```

One person's 50x long is their problem. A million users' 50x longs is systemic risk.

**Mitigations:**
- Lower max leverage (Solana perps cap at 10-20x vs 100x elsewhere)
- Gradual liquidation (25% at a time)
- Circuit breakers during extreme moves
- Open interest limits

### Insurance Fund & Bad Debt

Sometimes positions go underwater faster than liquidators can act. Margin is gone, but debt remains.

The insurance fund absorbs bad debt. When it's depleted, the protocol has two options:

1. **Socialize losses** across all users
2. **ADL (Auto-Deleveraging)**: Force-close profitable positions to cover the loss

ADL is the nuclear option. You're up 50% on a winning trade, then suddenly closed out because someone else went underwater. Unfair, but it prevents insolvency.

```rust
pub fn adl_priority(position: &Position, price: u64) -> u64 {
    let pnl = calculate_pnl(position, price);
    if pnl <= 0 { return 0; }
    
    // Higher profit + higher leverage = first to be ADL'd
    let ratio = margin_ratio(position, price);
    pnl as u64 * PRECISION / ratio
}
```

Traders hate ADL. But it's better than the protocol going insolvent.
